# Measurable dietary criteria for scoring dinners: (1) weight reduction, (2) muscle building / high protein

## Weight loss: deficit, dinner kcal share, energy density, protein in deficit, fibre, ultra-processed food

### Takeaway
The strongest measurable levers for a single dinner are: total kcal relative to a daily target, energy density (kcal/g) and protein per meal. Across a week, the levers are the average kcal against target, the share of ultra-processed meals and fibre. The Hall 2019 RCT gives a concrete effect size: about +500 kcal/day of spontaneous intake on an ultra-processed diet.

### Cited Findings
- Hall et al. 2019 (Cell Metabolism), an inpatient crossover RCT with n=20 (10 men, 10 women), 2 weeks per diet, food ad libitum. Participants ate about 500 kcal/day more on the ultra-processed diet. They gained about 0.9 kg on it and lost a similar amount on the unprocessed diet. The diets were matched for presented calories, sugars, fibre, fat and carbohydrate. Protein was not exactly matched, and the authors say the small protein difference could explain up to half of the intake gap. — [NIH news release](https://www.nih.gov/news-events/news-releases/nih-study-finds-heavily-processed-foods-cause-overeating-weight-gain)
- Energy density by macronutrient: water 0 kcal/g, fibre 1.5–2.5 kcal/g, protein and carbohydrate 4 kcal/g, fat 9 kcal/g. — [Aprifel](https://aprifel.com/en/global-fv-newsletter-article/the-importance-of-energy-density-in-weight-management)
- One-year Rolls trial: two daily servings of low-energy-dense soup produced 7.2 kg weight loss, against 4.8 kg with high-energy-dense dry snacks (about 50% more). In Ello-Martin et al. (1 year), adding fruit and vegetables plus reduced fat gave 7.9 kg loss against 6.4 kg for fat reduction alone (about 23% more), with less reported hunger. — [Aprifel](https://aprifel.com/en/global-fv-newsletter-article/the-importance-of-energy-density-in-weight-management); see also [Rolls, Dietary energy density in the treatment of obesity, year-long trial](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/dietary-energy-density-in-the-treatment-of-obesity-a-year-long-tr/)
- Helms et al. 2014 is a review of protein intake for weight loss in resistance-trained athletes. Its recommended range is 2.3–3.1 g/kg of fat-free mass per day during an energy deficit. — [PubMed 25014731](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25014731/) (only the search snippet was seen; the abstract was not fetched)
- The ISSN 2017 text says higher protein intakes (2–3× the RDA) combined with energy restriction support fat loss while preserving lean mass. This is discussion in the text, not a numbered statement. — [ISSN position stand, Jäger 2017](https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8)

### Inferences
- **Daily deficit for scoring:** use about 500 kcal/day below TDEE, which is roughly 20–25% of TDEE for an adult man at about 2,500–2,800 kcal. This is the common clinical convention (roughly 0.5 kg/week). It was **not verified from a primary guideline** in this session (see Gaps).
- **Dinner share:** dinner typically accounts for about 25–35% of daily kcal. For a man on about 2,000–2,200 kcal in a deficit, that gives a dinner target of about 550–750 kcal. The suggested ranges below are design choices, not cited norms:
  - Ideal: within ±15% of the per-person dinner target.
  - Yellow flag: more than 115% of target, or about 900 kcal or more for an adult man.
  - Red flag: more than 140% of target, or about 1,100 kcal or more, which on its own would use about half of a reduction day's budget.
- **Energy density (kcal per g of prepared dish):**
  - Good: below 1.5 kcal/g.
  - Yellow flag: 1.5–2.25 kcal/g.
  - Red flag: above 2.25 kcal/g.
  These are the Rolls "Volumetrics" bands. The bands are widely used, but no fetched source in this session confirmed them.
- **Weekly ultra-processed food:** a red flag when 3 or more of 7 dinners are NOVA-4 based (ready meals, processed meat substitutes as the main component, instant products). This is an inference from the +500 kcal/day effect.
- **Protein in a deficit:** score protein higher in reduction mode, for example a daily target of 1.6–2.2 g/kg of body weight, or 2.3–3.1 g/kg of fat-free mass (Helms) for trained people.

### Gaps
- I could not fetch the EASO 2015/2019 or WHO guideline text to confirm the 500–1,000 kcal/day deficit or the 0.5–1 kg/week figures. Searches returned only secondary pages.
- No primary source was fetched for the EFSA fibre adequate intake (commonly quoted as 25 g/day for adults). Treat it as unverified here. A fibre-per-dinner target of 8–10 g or more (about one third of 25–30 g) is an inference.
- No validated "dinner share of daily kcal" norm was found. Any such share in the app is a design parameter.

## Muscle building: ISSN protein targets, per-meal dose, leucine, distribution, pre-sleep protein, plant protein quality

### Takeaway
For a single dinner, protein should be about 0.25–0.4 g/kg (roughly 20–40 g, toward the upper end for plant sources) with about 2–3 g of leucine. Across a day, the target is 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day (gains plateau around 1.6 g/kg/day on average, with a 95% CI upper bound of about 2.2 g/kg). Dinner can also act as the pre-sleep protein dose (30–40 g).

### Cited Findings
- ISSN position stand statements (Jäger et al. 2017):
  - (2) 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day is sufficient for most exercising people.
  - (3) More than 3.0 g/kg/day may improve body composition in resistance-trained people.
  - (4) About 0.25 g/kg of high-quality protein per dose, or an absolute 20–40 g.
  - (5) 700–3,000 mg of leucine per dose, plus a balanced array of essential amino acids.
  - (6) Doses spread evenly every 3–4 h.
  - (13) Pre-sleep casein (30–40 g) increases overnight muscle protein synthesis.
  — [ISSN, Jäger 2017](https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8)
- Morton et al. 2018 meta-analysis (BJSM): the breakpoint for fat-free mass gains is 1.62 g/kg/day, with a 95% CI of 1.03–2.20 g/kg. The breakpoint itself was not significant (p=0.079). Pooled fat-free mass gain from protein supplementation was 1.05 kg in trained people (95% CI 0.61–1.50) and 0.15 kg (not significant) in untrained people. — [Stronger by Science summary](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/?p=61316); [McMaster news](https://science.mcmaster.ca/protein-supplements-work-to-a-point/)
- The DIAAS overview of plant and animal proteins (Herreman et al. 2020) is the reference for quality adjustments. Its full text was blocked by a captcha, so no values were extracted. — [PMC7590266](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7590266)

### Inferences
- **Per-dinner protein for a man of 85–95 kg:**
  - Target: 0.25 g/kg × 90 = about 22 g minimum. Practically 30–40 g, because dinner doubles as the pre-sleep dose (ISSN statement 13).
  - Yellow flag: below 25 g.
  - Red flag: below 20 g.
  - For plant-only meals, add about 10–20% (or multiply by 1/DIAAS) and aim for at least 2.5 g of leucine. Plant proteins generally have lower leucine content and digestibility than animal proteins. This is a general consensus point; no DIAAS values were confirmed in this session.
- **Daily (weekly average) protein:**
  - Target: 1.6 g/kg (about 145 g for 90 kg).
  - Yellow flag: below 1.4 g/kg.
  - Red flag: below 1.2 g/kg.
  - Anything above about 2.2 g/kg brings no added muscle benefit (top of the Morton CI).
  - In a deficit, raise the target to 1.8–2.2 g/kg of body weight.
- **Weekly distribution:** penalise days when the dinner provides less than about 25% of daily protein. Penalise weeks with high variance (for example 2 or more dinners below 20 g).
- **Woman (example 60–65 kg):** scale by body mass. 0.25–0.4 g/kg gives about 15–25 g minimum and 25–30 g ideal per dinner, with the same 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day when training.

## Per-person targets: man training vs woman vs child 2–4 years

### Takeaway
Express all protein targets in g/kg and multiply by each person's body weight. Children need far less protein per kg than trained adults, and an adult "high-protein" dinner portion is not appropriate for a toddler.

### Cited Findings
- EFSA protein population reference intake (PRI) for children, in g/kg/day: 1 year 1.14; 1.5 years 1.03; 2 years 0.97; 3 years 0.90; 4 years 0.86. For example, a 14 kg 3-year-old needs about 12.6 g/day. — [EU Knowledge4Policy (EFSA 2012)](https://knowledge4policy.ec.europa.eu/health-promotion-knowledge-gateway/dietary-protein-recommendations-children-5_en)
- The adult EFSA PRI of 0.83 g/kg/day is widely quoted but was not confirmed on the fetched page. — [NutritionInsight on EFSA PRI](https://www.nutritioninsight.com/news/efsa-sets-population-reference-intakes-for-protein.html) (not fetched)

### Inferences
- **Child aged 2–4 (about 12–16 kg):** daily protein is about 11–15 g, so dinner needs only about 3–5 g, which is easily met. Score the child on adequacy of protein, not on maximising it. Flag very large protein or very salty and processed portions rather than low protein. Energy for this age is roughly 1,000–1,300 kcal/day (EFSA 2013 average requirements; not verified here), so a dinner of about 250–350 kcal fits.
- **Scoring formula idea:** meal protein score = min(10, 10 × protein_g / (target_g_per_kg × body weight)), with flags as above. Weekly score = mean of meal scores, minus penalties for the number of red-flag meals and the NOVA-4 count.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve EFSA energy average requirements for children aged 1–4 or for adults, or the EFSA fibre adequate intake for children (often quoted as 10 g/day at 1–3 years).
- No numeric DIAAS values were extracted because the PMC page was blocked.
- No sources from 2023–2026 were checked for updates. The ISSN 2017 statements remain the standard reference, as far as I know.
